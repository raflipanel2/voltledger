FIELD NOTE 14 / INVESTIGATIONS

A practical standby-power audit for a typical home

Find possible “phantom loads” without turning off equipment that matters.

By VoltLedger Editorial · Prepared 11 November 2026 · Educational estimate

Standby power is electricity a device can use while not performing its main function. A good audit treats standby loads as candidates to measure, not proof that every plugged-in device is wasteful.

Make the inventory before moving plugs

Walk through rooms and list devices that remain connected when apparently off. Record their purpose: clocks, remote wake-up, recording schedules, updates, security, and medical services are legitimate reasons to stay powered.

Start with a small set of optional accessories rather than trying to unplug the entire home. A simple table with device, stated standby watts, hours/day, and importance gives the audit a useful structure.

Measure small loads responsibly

An appropriate plug-in electricity meter may show standby wattage for compatible devices, but low readings can fluctuate and some meters struggle with very small loads. Repeated readings help, as does checking the meter’s accuracy limits.

Do not plug permanently wired appliances, medical equipment, or devices exceeding the meter’s ratings into a consumer meter. Never open electrical equipment to obtain a reading. Manufacturer data is preferable where measurement would be unsafe.

Turn findings into safe actions

For a nonessential device genuinely drawing 4 W continuously, the yearly model is 4/1000 × 24 × 365 = 35.04 kWh. That number tells you the energy magnitude, but whether to cut it depends on the service it provides and how much effort it takes.

Try a documented power-save setting or a suitable switched strip where doing so is safe. Confirm there are no lost recordings, reset clocks, disrupted updates, or other problems before claiming an improvement.

APPLIED EXAMPLE / NOT A UNIVERSAL RESULT

Work through the numbers

Four optional accessories with measured standby draws of 1 W, 2 W, 2 W and 3 W total 8 W. If those draws were continuous, they would use about 70.1 kWh/year. The audit should still mark which devices need constant power.

A small field checklist

Use these actions before making a purchase, changing a schedule or explaining an unexpectedly high bill. Keep a record of the assumptions that matter.

  1. Write down each device’s actual function while idle.
  2. Prefer measured watts or credible manufacturer figures.
  3. Mark “must stay on” before changing power.
  4. Calculate kWh/year and verify a change actually reduces draw.
Common mistake

A standby-load estimate is a decision aid, not a recommendation to disconnect necessary equipment.
