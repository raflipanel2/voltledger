FIELD NOTE 04 / BUYING WISELY

How to read an EnergyGuide label before buying

Compare annual kWh, assumptions, and features—not only a bold yearly dollar amount.

By VoltLedger Editorial · Prepared 11 November 2026 · Educational estimate

For many appliances sold in the United States, the yellow EnergyGuide label helps shoppers compare estimated energy use among models. It is a standardized comparison tool, not a personalized prediction.

Understand the difference between cost and energy

An EnergyGuide label may show estimated yearly operating cost and a comparison range for similar products. Those estimates use standardized conditions and an assumed energy price. Your household usage, climate, and utility tariff may be different.

If the label provides annual kWh, multiply that number by your own energy-only price per kWh for a more useful local starting point. Keep in mind that taxes, fees, seasonal behavior, and local tariff structures can still make the real bill different.

Compare the same category and usable features

Two refrigerators with different sizes, freezer arrangements, or automatic features do not provide the same service. Before celebrating the lower kWh figure, make sure you are comparing capacity, performance, interior layout, and features you actually need.

ENERGY STAR is a separate voluntary efficiency certification. A product may display the mark alongside an EnergyGuide label if it qualifies; the two labels answer different questions. Do not infer that every EnergyGuide product has ENERGY STAR certification.

Turn the label into a decision

Record purchase price, expected years owned, annual energy kWh, your current tariff, and likely maintenance or repair differences. Calculate annual energy cost and a simple total over a horizon. Keep the purchase-price premium separate from the energy savings so the tradeoff stays visible.

A lower energy number does not automatically justify replacing a functioning product immediately. Embodied impacts, usable life, repair, and affordability matter; the label provides a comparison input, not a command to buy.

APPLIED EXAMPLE / NOT A UNIVERSAL RESULT

Work through the numbers

Model A uses 420 kWh/year and Model B uses 310 kWh/year. Their estimated difference is 110 kWh/year. At $0.22/kWh, that is $24.20/year in variable energy charges. If B costs $400 more, electricity savings alone would take more than 16 years to offset the difference, before considering rate or repair changes.

A small field checklist

Use these actions before making a purchase, changing a schedule or explaining an unexpectedly high bill. Keep a record of the assumptions that matter.

  1. Compare similar capacity and features.
  2. Use yearly kWh when possible.
  3. Calculate costs using the local energy-only rate.
  4. Document assumptions before choosing a more expensive unit.
Common mistake

EnergyGuide operating costs are estimates and can diverge materially from your actual bill.
