Estimate an appliance’s electricity cost step by step

Turn nameplate watts and actual use time into an estimate you can explain.

By VoltLedger Editorial · Prepared 11 November 2026 · Educational estimate

When deciding whether a device is expensive to run, begin with a transparent calculation rather than an online “average cost.” The useful number depends on your wattage, schedule, quantity, and tariff.

Start with a transparent formula

For a device with approximately steady draw, estimated kWh = watts ÷ 1,000 × hours per day × days used × number of units. Estimated energy cost = kWh × energy price per kWh. This is the core method used in educational appliance-energy worksheets.

Select one consistent period. For a month, 30 days is a convenient modeling assumption rather than a promise that the billing statement has exactly 30 days. For seasonal appliances, use actual days of operation per year instead of multiplying a winter schedule across all twelve months.

A nameplate can overstate typical consumption

A laptop charger rated 65 W does not necessarily draw 65 W continuously; charging power changes as the battery fills. A refrigerator cycles. A washing machine changes operating modes. Treat rated wattage as a starting point and replace it with measured or manufacturer-specified kWh when available.

If a device has a known duty ratio, the simulator can reduce the assumed active time. However, inserting a 40% duty assumption does not turn the estimate into a measurement. Write that assumption down so the resulting number is not mistaken for fact.

Know what the estimate leaves out

Most simple calculators omit taxes, fixed utility fees, minimum charges, varying marginal rates, and power factor questions. They also do not account for weather, device aging, software settings, or other household equipment. These exclusions matter when comparing the output with an actual invoice.

Use an estimate to rank possible investigations. To verify substantial energy spending, compare several bills and use appropriate meters or appliance energy labels. Never use a DIY measurement to open, rewire, or modify mains electrical equipment.

APPLIED EXAMPLE / NOT A UNIVERSAL RESULT

Work through the numbers

A 75 W fan used 8 hours on 24 days consumes 75 × 8 × 24 ÷ 1,000 = 14.4 kWh. At $0.25/kWh the estimated variable energy cost is $3.60 for those uses. The bill can differ because of fees, taxes, or time-varying rates.

A small field checklist

Use these actions before making a purchase, changing a schedule or explaining an unexpectedly high bill. Keep a record of the assumptions that matter.

  1. Find the device’s actual rated or measured power.
  2. Record a realistic schedule for the period considered.
  3. Keep currency and price units explicit.
  4. Recalculate with low and high use estimates to see sensitivity.
Common mistake

A single number is less useful than a range with assumptions you can audit.
