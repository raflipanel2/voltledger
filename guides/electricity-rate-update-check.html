When your electricity rate changes: a verification workflow

Use the utility’s published tariff, not screenshots or viral rate claims.

By VoltLedger Editorial · Prepared 11 November 2026 · Educational estimate

Electricity prices are a moving input in every running-cost calculator. A screenshot of a rate announcement can become outdated or omit fees, seasonal periods, and eligibility rules. This reference workflow explains how to verify a real change.

Identify the correct provider and tariff

Start with your most recent invoice, which usually lists the account’s rate plan or tariff code. Find the utility’s official tariff sheet or customer notice for that exact plan and service location. Avoid copying a national average when your own contract has a different structure.

Some plans feature a flat energy rate; others combine time-of-use prices, delivery components, fixed fees, and adjustments. Read the effective date carefully and confirm whether a published figure includes taxes or network costs.

Update calculations with a dated source

Write the new energy-only price, its unit, effective date, and source URL beside your calculations. If the provider announces future changes, keep current and future scenarios separate.

A cost estimator should not silently pretend to know live local electricity prices. In VoltLedger’s tools, the tariff field is editable precisely because the site cannot verify your account or bill.

Check the impact on earlier decisions

Recalculate any appliance payback or load estimate that depended heavily on the old rate. A higher rate increases modeled savings from using less energy, but fixed fees and time-of-use periods still matter.

Keep the old calculation as a snapshot rather than replacing history. If a plan change is optional, compare the whole expected household month, not only the marketing headline for the cheapest time window.

APPLIED EXAMPLE / NOT A UNIVERSAL RESULT

Work through the numbers

If a tariff rises from $0.18 to $0.23/kWh, a 100 kWh/month energy-use estimate changes from $18 to $23 in variable energy charges. That $5 difference excludes other changes in the bill and should not be described as an actual forecast.

A small field checklist

Use these actions before making a purchase, changing a schedule or explaining an unexpectedly high bill. Keep a record of the assumptions that matter.

  1. Find the tariff code on your current invoice.
  2. Verify the utility’s official effective date and units.
  3. Record whether fees and taxes are included.
  4. Recalculate earlier scenarios using dated assumptions.
Common mistake

This is a method article, not a claim that any specific utility changed rates today.
