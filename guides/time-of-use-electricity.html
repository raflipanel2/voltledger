FIELD NOTE 18 / INVESTIGATIONS

Time-of-use electricity rates: separate timing from total energy

Why moving a task can change cost without changing kWh.

By VoltLedger Editorial · Prepared 11 November 2026 · Educational estimate

Under a time-of-use tariff, the price of a kWh depends on when it is consumed. The same appliance may use exactly the same energy on Tuesday afternoon and Tuesday evening, yet incur a different energy charge.

Read the utility’s actual schedule

Find the published rate periods for weekdays, weekends, holidays, and seasons. Do not assume off-peak means “after 10 p.m.” everywhere. Different utilities may change the rate windows or create extra critical-peak periods.

A tariff may include delivery charges, demand charges, minimum fees, or credits that a simple two-rate model cannot capture. Keep this context when evaluating claims about shifting usage.

Calculate the same kWh under two rates

If a task consumes 1.2 kWh, the energy-only charge at $0.35/kWh is $0.42 and at $0.15/kWh is $0.18. The modeled difference is $0.24 for that task. The calculation is useful even if the task itself becomes no more energy efficient.

Not every task can or should move. Family routines, building noise rules, safety, equipment requirements, and accessibility may limit schedule changes. A dishwasher timer is only sensible if the manufacturer supports that mode and the household considers it safe.

Compare the total result, not the headline rate

Some time-of-use plans have lower prices during certain hours but higher prices during busy periods. Simulate the whole household’s likely distribution rather than multiplying the total monthly kWh by the cheapest advertised rate.

If a plan change is available, account for plan fees, billing minimums, and contract terms. A rate plan that saves one small task may cost more overall if your household has significant peak use.

APPLIED EXAMPLE / NOT A UNIVERSAL RESULT

Work through the numbers

Assume a task uses 2 kWh. Under a peak price of $0.34/kWh it costs $0.68 in energy charges. Under an off-peak price of $0.16/kWh it costs $0.32. Shifting it saves $0.36 in this example, with no kWh reduction.

A small field checklist

Use these actions before making a purchase, changing a schedule or explaining an unexpectedly high bill. Keep a record of the assumptions that matter.

  1. Verify time windows and seasonal rules with the utility.
  2. Compute kWh multiplied by each relevant rate.
  3. Consider realistic household scheduling constraints.
  4. Compare total monthly economics before changing plans.
Common mistake

Time-of-use savings are about the price of when energy is used, not necessarily reducing total energy.
